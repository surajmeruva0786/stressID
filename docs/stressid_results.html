Benchmark report · binary stress detection

Where our StressID model actually stands

A like-for-like comparison against every published result on StressID, plus the control experiments that decide how much any of these numbers are worth.

700 recordings · 64 participants 15-fold nested CV Held-out partitions, seed 101

01 The short answer

Is this a state-of-the-art result?

On the headline metric, yes — narrowly. As a scientific claim, no, and it should not be written up that way. Our best configuration reaches 0.749 weighted F1 where the StressID benchmark paper reports 0.72 for its best fusion model. But the margin is about 0.02, the two protocols are similar rather than identical, and — decisively — a classifier given nothing but recording length and which sensors were switched on scores 0.709 on the same data.

Essentially the entire published field on this benchmark, ours included, sits within a few points of a baseline that reads no signal at all. That, rather than the leaderboard position, is the finding worth reporting.

What our pipeline does demonstrably earn is measured against a matched baseline rather than a published one: run a plain random forest through the same folds and it scores 0.723, so the full model is worth +0.026, p = 0.002. Real, significant, and smaller than the confound it sits on top of.

Read this before the tables

Because the same participant appears on both sides of the split, every number in sections 3 and 4 is inflated by subject leakage. We measured that separately: physiological features alone identify which of 64 participants a recording came from with 64.9% accuracy, 41.5× chance. We keep the leaky protocol only because it is the one the published numbers use. Under a participant-disjoint split the same pipeline scores about 0.52.

Does the pipeline earn its keep?

Rows in the table above come from two different split rules, so they cannot be subtracted from one another. The honest test is a plain random forest run through the identical folds, seed and scoring as our final model:

Full pipeline 0.748 vs plain random forest 0.723 — a gain of +0.026, paired p = 0.002, winning 12 of 15 folds. On the confound-free subset the gap widens to +0.037 (p = 0.013). The engineering is doing real work; it is simply doing it on top of a base that protocol metadata already supplies.

On the full corpus, everything our pipeline learns from physiology, voice and face is worth +0.040 over knowing how long the recording was and which sensors were on. The benchmark paper's headline 0.72 sits below that metadata ceiling.

05 The subset where the confounds vanish

364 of the 700 recordings carry all three modalities. Within that subset every recording is the same length and every sensor is present, so duration and availability carry exactly zero information — a task-identity classifier there scores 0.418, which is the majority baseline.

Recommendation for the write-up

Report the 700-recording number for comparability with published work, placed immediately beside the metadata baseline that shows what it is worth. Report the 364-recording result — 0.675 macro F1, +0.257 over its ceiling — as the primary scientific finding. A reader shown only 0.749 will draw the wrong conclusion.

The regression is the most interpretable of the three. Predicting the mean rating for every recording gives RMSE 2.488; the model reaches 1.857, a 25% error reduction, explaining 44% of the variance in what participants said about their own stress. Pearson and Spearman agree closely (0.673 vs 0.660), so the relationship is monotonic rather than driven by a handful of extreme recordings.

Two negative results worth publishing

Expert features did not help. LF/HF ratio is the most cited autonomic stress marker in the literature, and the voice block is eGeMAPS-style. Both were physiologically sound, both discriminated stress individually (Cohen's d 0.36–0.42), and both were worth about two thousandths of macro F1 once the existing representation was present. The window-aggregate and log-mel features already encode what these descriptors encode.

Learned sequence models lost to trees. Given a fair test — two architectures, identical inner folds — the ensemble assigned the GPU sequence models 0.7% of its weight, and zero weight in three of five folds. At 700 recordings, representation learning does not pay on this dataset. The same result now holds on two different evaluation protocols here.

08 Limitations to state explicitly

  • Subject leakage inflates every number in sections 3–4. The protocol is the published one, and it lets the same participant appear on both sides of the split. A participant-disjoint evaluation of the same pipeline gives roughly 0.52.
  • Search bias is measured at +0.014. Development used one fixed partition; re-running the frozen configuration on partitions never touched during the search drops macro F1 from 0.760 to 0.748. The lower figure is the one reported throughout.
  • Protocols are comparable, not identical. The benchmark uses a random 80/20 split with SMOTE; ours uses stratified 5-fold without. The right phrasing is “competitive under a comparable protocol”, not a like-for-like win.
  • Two normalisations are transductive. Participant-relative and participant-z features read no labels, but test rows contribute to their own participant's statistics. Admissible only because the protocol already shares participants across the split.
  • Our jitter, shimmer and HNR are frame-level, not Praat's cycle-level definitions, and run on a different scale. They should not be reported as though interchangeable with published values.
